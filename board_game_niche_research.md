# Finding Under-Served Board Game Niches: Demand Without Supply

*Research deliverable — June 2026*

## The core idea you got right

You're looking for the **intersection of two circles**, not either one alone:

- **Demand proven** — people actively want games in this space (wishlists, sold-out reprints, over-funded campaigns, big fanbases in adjacent media).
- **Supply thin** — few titles exist, nothing is "the definitive one," or the good ones are old / out of print / poorly rated.

A theme that is *only* thin on supply is a trap — your own "monkey games" example. Nobody is making monkey games because nobody wants one. A theme that is *only* high-demand (fantasy, Cthulhu, zombies) is over-saturated — you'd be the 400th fantasy game. **The money is strictly in the overlap**, and the overlap is where this report points.

One honest caveat up front: hard, citable market data (BGG wishlist counts, secondary-market prices, fast-funding numbers) is genuinely hard to pull at scale for every theme. The verified anchor below is rock-solid; the candidate-niche list that follows is informed by the same logic plus established knowledge of the hobby, and each is flagged with how strong the signal is. The most durable thing in this document is the **repeatable method** (Section 4) — that's the engine you run forever, not a static list that goes stale.

## 1. The one fully-verified niche: world mythology beyond Greek/Norse

This is the clearest demand/supply gap the research could *prove* with hard numbers.

**Demand evidence (verified):** Bitewing Games' "Mythos Collection" deliberately mines distinct, less-common world mythologies. Their two-game Kickstarter for **Azure** (Chinese mythology — the Four Auspicious Beasts) and **Moytura** (Irish/Celtic mythology — Tuatha Dé Danann vs. Fomorians) raised **$200,313 from 2,107 backers on a $15,000 goal — roughly 13× over-funded.**

**Supply evidence:** Greek and Norse mythology are saturated (dozens of titles). Irish/Celtic, Chinese, Mesoamerican (Aztec/Maya), Polynesian/Pacific, Slavic, West African (Yoruba/Akan), Hindu, Mesopotamian, and Indigenous mythologies are barely touched by *good* strategy games. The publisher's own progression — start in saturated Greek, then move into under-served Irish — is a deliberate gap-mining strategy you can copy.

**Two important nuances (both verified):**
1. **This demand was captured at *light-to-mid* weight, not heavy.** Azure plays ~20 min, Moytura ~45 min, BGG weight ~2.25/5. Your passion is mid-to-heavy. So the proven demand is for *accessible* mythology games; whether a 3+ hour heavy mythology strategy game captures the *same* audience is an open question. It may capture a different (smaller, but real) one.
2. **Designer-reputation confound.** That campaign was led by the designers of *Undaunted*, *War Chest*, and *Mandala* — decorated names placed front-and-center in the campaign title. A first-time designer should **not** assume 13× over-funding from theme alone. The theme helped; the pedigree helped more. Plan your funding expectations accordingly.

**Takeaway:** Non-Greek/Norse mythology is a validated gap. Pick a culture with a passionate diaspora and a rich pantheon (Irish, Slavic, Yoruba, Aztec, Hindu, Polynesian) and you are entering proven-demand / thin-supply territory.

## 3. The most reliable gap type: proven mechanic × under-used theme

This is the single most actionable framing in the whole report, because it de-risks both sides at once. You take a mechanic that is *already proven to sell* and pair it with a theme that mechanic has never worn. The mechanic guarantees the gameplay audience; the fresh theme is your visible differentiator on a crowded shelf.

The recipe:
1. List the mechanics with proven commercial demand: **engine-building, deck-building, worker placement, area majority/control, network/route-building, legacy/campaign, tableau-building, deck-construction.**
2. List the themes those mechanics have *over-used*: fantasy, farming, trains, space 4X, medieval cities, zombies, Cthulhu, pirates, Wild West.
3. Find the empty cells. **A proven mechanic that has never been paired with an in-demand theme is your highest-confidence gap**, because you're not betting on whether the *gameplay* sells — only on whether the *skin* is fresh.

Worked examples (validate each, but the logic is sound):
- **Engine-building × industrial labor history** (you're not farming or building birds — you're running a 1900s factory floor and managing a workforce).
- **Network/route-building × Indian Ocean or Swahili-coast trade** (the *Ticket to Ride* / *Brass* canal-network feel, on a map nobody has used).
- **Legacy campaign × non-Greek mythology** (the *Pandemic Legacy* structure applied to a Slavic or Yoruba pantheon war).
- **Area majority × ecology** (the *El Grande* / *Blood Rage* control loop, but it's a coral reef or a fungal network competing for territory).
- **Deck-building × real working systems** (run a hospital, a port, or an air-traffic tower as an evolving deck).

Why this beats chasing a pure "untouched theme": an untouched theme might be untouched because the gameplay underneath doesn't work or doesn't sell. A *proven mechanic* removes that risk — you already know people will buy and play that engine. You're only innovating on the wrapper.

## 4. The repeatable method — your gap-finding engine

This is the durable deliverable. A static list goes stale in a year; this process you run forever. There are two layers: a **data layer** (quantify demand and supply) and an **analysis layer** (find clusters of appeal that themes haven't filled).

### 4a. The data layer — fields to track, and how to read them

**On BoardGameGeek (your demand + supply gauge):**
- **"Wishlist" + "Want in trade" counts** (on every game's Stats page). High wishlist + few existing titles in that theme = demand outrunning supply. This is your single best "people want this" field.
- **OOP (out-of-print) status + secondary-market price.** A game selling for 2–4× its retail on the aftermarket is a screaming "demand exceeds supply" signal. Cross-reference with reprint-request threads.
- **Rating vs. number of ratings.** A theme where the *few* existing games rate highly (7.5+) but have *low* rating counts = quality demand, low awareness/availability = room to be "the one."
- **Category/theme browse + game count.** Browse by theme tag and count titles. Compare "how many games" against "how much do people want them." Few games + high wishlists on the ones that exist = gap.
- **Forum threads:** search "underused theme," "is there a game about X," "themes you'd love to see," and reprint-request threads. These are raw, unfiltered demand. (One verified caveat: forums are sentiment, not hard data — use them to *generate* hypotheses, then confirm with the numbers above.)

**On Kickstarter / Gamefound (your funded-demand gauge):**
- Sort the tabletop category by **"Most Funded"** and filter by theme. A theme that *over-funds consistently* but has *few campaigns* is a live gap.
- Watch **funding velocity** — funded in hours/first day = strong demand. Tools like Tabletop Analytics / Kickstarter-tracking sites let you sort by dollars raised and velocity.
- Note the **goal-vs-raised multiple**. The mythology example (13× goal) is the kind of ratio you're hunting — but always discount for designer reputation (Section 1's confound).

**On Google Trends / search:** rising search interest in a theme (a TV show, a mythology, a hobby) with no good board game equivalent = crossover demand you can capture first.

### 4b. The analysis layer — cluster by "core appeal," not by label (verified method)

The most powerful technique the research surfaced comes from Dinesh Vatvani's BGG analysis (dvatvani.github.io/BGG-Analysis-Part-3.html), built on **7.5 million individual user ratings across 553 games from 265,374 accounts.**

The key insight: **don't classify games by their mechanic *labels* — classify them by what audiences actually feel.** If the same users tend to love two games, those games share a "core appeal" even if their mechanic tags look totally different. His map clusters the hobby by appeals like *abstract puzzle, thematic immersion, social interaction, engine-building, cooperation* — and reveals similarities that the official taxonomy completely hides.

**How you use this to find gaps:**
1. Identify the high-appeal cluster you want to serve (e.g., "deep engine-building immersion" — the *Terraforming Mars* / *Gaia Project* itch).
2. List the themes that already occupy that cluster (space, civilization, nature).
3. **Look for an in-demand theme that scratches that *same itch* but has no representative in the cluster.** That empty cell — high-appeal cluster, no good thematic occupant — is a precise, data-grounded gap.

**One honest limitation (verified):** this method maps which games are *similar*, not directly which themes are *under-supplied*. You add the gap-interpretation layer yourself by overlaying it with the BGG demand fields in 4a (wishlist counts, title counts per theme). The method finds the *appeal clusters*; the BGG fields tell you which clusters are *thirsty*.

## 5. The saturated baseline — what to avoid (or radically re-skin)

So the boundary is unambiguous, here is the confirmed over-saturated set. Entering these means fighting hundreds of competitors:

- **From your brief + community consensus:** fantasy, farming/agriculture, zombies, Cthulhu/Lovecraft, trains/railroads, space 4X, medieval city-building.
- **From BGG community threads (verified, medium confidence):** Renaissance, Egypt, the Middle Ages, the Caribbean/pirates, German trading companies, ancient China and Japan, generic "animals," racing, and most major world cities.

These aren't *forbidden* — but in a saturated theme you must win on **mechanics or production**, because the theme buys you nothing. In an under-served theme, the theme itself is your marketing. Given that you want to self-publish and crowdfund (where the *hook* drives the campaign), the under-served route plays directly to your distribution model.

## 6. What to do next — concrete steps

1. **Pick 3 candidate niches** from Sections 1–3 that genuinely excite *you* (passion shows in design, and you'll be living with it for years). The mythology gap is your safest validated starting point.
2. **Run each through Section 4a** — pull the BGG wishlist/OOP/rating numbers and the Kickstarter funded-velocity data. Kill any niche where demand is weak. Keep the ones where demand is real and supply is thin.
3. **Frame each survivor as a mechanic × theme pair** (Section 3) so your gameplay risk is low and your thematic differentiation is high.
4. **Build the gap-finder as a standing habit**, not a one-off. A simple spreadsheet — columns for theme, # of existing titles, top title's rating, top title's wishlist count, best campaign's funded-multiple, OOP/aftermarket signal — turns this whole report into a dashboard you update monthly. The gaps move; the method doesn't.
